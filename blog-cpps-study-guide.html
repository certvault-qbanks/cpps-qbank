How to Pass the CPPS Exam in 2026: Complete Study Guide

A domain-by-domain breakdown, recommended study timeline, and strategies for every CPPS content area.

The Certified Professional in Patient Safety (CPPS) exam doesn't reward people who can recite definitions. It rewards people who can look at a messy clinical scenario and pick the response a well-run safety program would choose. That changes how you should prepare.

This guide walks through how the exam is built, how the four content domains are weighted, what to study in each one, and a week-by-week plan you can actually follow alongside a full-time job.

The CPPS exam at a glance

The CPPS credential is awarded by the Certification Board for Professionals in Patient Safety (CBPPS). The Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI) is the program's home, and PSI Services delivers the exam. Here's what the current candidate handbook describes:

ItemDetails
Questions120 multiple-choice questions, four answer options each
Scored questions100. The other 20 are unscored pretest items mixed in with no marking
Time150 minutes, with no scheduled breaks
WherePSI test centers or live remote proctoring
Question styleMostly application and analysis; only a small share is pure recall
ScoringPass/fail against a fixed standard (not a curve). No penalty for guessing
Fee$549 in the U.S.; $649 for international candidates
RenewalEvery 3 years, through 45 continuing education hours or a retest

The number that matters most: 75 seconds

150 minutes divided by 120 questions gives you about 75 seconds per question. You can't tell which 20 items are unscored, so every question has to be treated as if it counts. Practicing under a timer isn't optional for this exam.

Are you eligible?

CBPPS requires that patient safety be an integral part of your current or future job responsibilities, plus one of these education and experience combinations:

  • Bachelor's degree or higher and 3 years of experience in a healthcare setting (or with a company that serves the healthcare industry)
  • Associate degree or equivalent and 5 years of that same experience

Two points people often miss. First, the experience doesn't have to be in patient safety; it has to be in healthcare. Nurses, pharmacists, clinical engineers, health IT analysts and quality staff can all qualify. Second, IHI counts time spent in clinical rotations and residency programs toward the experience requirement. You don't need a clinical license.

Applications are self-attested, and CBPPS audits a random sample each year, so keep documentation of your degree and work history handy.

The four domains, and where the points are

The current content outline (published September 2024) organizes the exam into four domains. Because exactly 100 questions are scored, the item count for each domain is also its percentage of your score.

Safety Risks and Responses35 items (35%)
Performance Measurement, Analysis, Improvement and Monitoring25 items (25%)
Culture20 items (20%)
Systems Thinking, Human Factors Engineering, and Design20 items (20%)

Look at that distribution before you plan anything. Safety Risks and Responses plus Performance Measurement make up 60% of your score. Many candidates spend most of their time on culture and leadership because that's what "patient safety" feels like it should be about. The blueprint says otherwise: the exam leans heavily toward finding risk, recognizing harm, analyzing events, and proving that a fix worked.

What to study in each domain

Domain 3: Safety Risks and Responses (35 items)

This is the biggest domain and the only one split into four subareas: risk assessment, risk and harm recognition, barriers to safety, and safety responses. Focus on:

  • High-risk areas: diagnostic error, medication use, procedures and surgery, patient deterioration, healthcare-acquired conditions and infections, transitions of care, and emergency preparedness.
  • Severity and escalation: how events are classified, when they must be escalated, and how reporting systems work, including external reporting of serious events.
  • Barriers: staffing and financial limits, supply shortages, interoperability problems, and gaps in access, language and health literacy.
  • Responses: checklists and protocols, rapid response systems, huddles and debriefs, de-escalation, and communication-and-resolution programs after harm.
  • Workforce safety: workplace violence, incivility, and psychological safety. The outline treats harm to staff as core content, not a side topic.

Domain 4: Performance Measurement, Analysis, Improvement and Monitoring (25 items)

This is where candidates without a quality-improvement background tend to lose points. Make sure you can:

  • Tell structure, process, outcome and balancing measures apart and pick the right one for a scenario. A balancing measure checks whether your fix caused a problem somewhere else.
  • Read a run chart or control chart and distinguish common-cause variation (normal noise) from special-cause variation (a real signal worth acting on).
  • Explain the Model for Improvement and PDSA cycles, including why you test small before spreading.
  • Choose between root cause analysis (serious events), apparent cause analysis (lower-severity events), and FMEA (proactive, before anything goes wrong).
  • Apply the hierarchy of action strength. Forcing functions, standardization and design changes beat education, new policies and "reminding staff to be careful."

Domain 1: Culture (20 items)

  • Just culture: distinguish human error (console the person, fix the system), at-risk behavior (coach and remove the incentive to drift), and reckless behavior (accountability).
  • Safety culture measurement: how validated culture and climate surveys are chosen, interpreted, and shared back with staff. This shows up often in the task list.
  • Psychological safety, teamwork and communication tools, learning systems, and how leaders respond after an event.
  • Leadership and outreach at the organizational, team and community level, including engaging patients and families.

Domain 2: Systems Thinking, Human Factors Engineering, and Design (20 items)

  • Named frameworks: the outline specifically lists SEIPS, Safety-I and Safety-II, and high reliability. Know each well enough to apply it.
  • High reliability principles: preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify, sensitivity to operations, commitment to resilience, and deference to expertise.
  • Human factors: cognitive bias, fatigue and workload limits, and why systems should be designed around how people actually work.
  • Design: usability testing, workflow, equipment and technology, and cost versus benefit when choosing a solution.

An 8-week study plan

Most working professionals do well with 6 to 10 weeks of steady preparation. This plan assumes about 6 to 8 hours per week and front-loads the heaviest domains.

WeekFocusGoal for the week
1BaselineRead the official content outline. Take a 50-question mixed set untimed and note your weakest domain.
2Safety Risks and Responses, part 1Risk assessment, harm recognition, high-risk areas. Do 40+ domain questions.
3Safety Risks and Responses, part 2Barriers, safety responses, workforce safety, disclosure. Do 40+ domain questions.
4Performance MeasurementMeasure types, run and control charts, RCA vs. apparent cause vs. FMEA, action hierarchy.
5CultureJust culture, culture surveys, psychological safety, leadership response to events.
6Systems, Human Factors and DesignSEIPS, Safety-I/II, high reliability, cognitive bias, usability.
7Full timed simulation120 questions in 150 minutes, no breaks. Review every miss and every lucky guess.
8Targeted reviewDrill your two weakest domains. Second timed simulation 3 to 5 days before the exam. Rest the day before.

If you're short on time, compress weeks 2 through 6 but never skip the timed simulations. Pacing problems are hard to fix on exam day.

Test-taking strategy

Think like the safety program, not the individual

When a question describes an error, the best answer almost always addresses the system that allowed it, not the person who made it. If one option is "retrain the nurse" and another is "redesign the process so the error can't happen," the redesign is usually right. Save the accountability answers for scenarios that clearly describe reckless behavior.

Look for the strongest action

Many questions boil down to "which intervention will work best and last longest?" Rank the options: eliminating the hazard or forcing the correct step beats simplifying and standardizing, which beats education, warnings and policy memos.

Watch the question's first word

"First," "next," "best" and "most important" change the answer. A question asking what to do first after serious harm is often about securing the patient's safety and supporting the people involved before analysis starts.

Pace in blocks

Aim to finish about 40 questions every 50 minutes. Flag anything you can't answer in 90 seconds, pick your best guess, and move on. There's no penalty for wrong answers, so never leave one blank.

Plan for no scratch paper if testing remotely

The handbook doesn't allow scratch paper under remote proctoring. If you're testing from home, practice reading control charts and working small calculations in your head.

Study resources worth your time

  • The official content outline and candidate handbook. Free, and the only documents that define what's tested. Read both first.
  • IHI's CPPS Review Course. Offered as live virtual sessions and an on-demand version with one year of access. IHI notes the course isn't required and doesn't guarantee a pass.
  • IHI's practice exam. Useful as a diagnostic checkpoint rather than a primary study tool.
  • A scenario-based question bank. Because the exam is mostly application questions, volume of realistic practice matters. CPPS Qbank has 500+ questions organized by domain, with explanations and a timed exam mode.
  • Scholarships. IHI's Lucian Leape Scholarship Program offers need-based support for first-time candidates, including review course access and an exam attempt. Check the IHI site for the next application window.

Test-day checklist

  • Confirm your appointment time and bring the ID exactly as named on your registration.
  • For remote testing, run the system check ahead of time and clear your desk completely.
  • Eat beforehand and use the restroom. There are no breaks.
  • Write down your pacing targets mentally: question 40 by 50 minutes, question 80 by 100 minutes.
  • Leave the last 10 minutes to revisit flagged questions.
