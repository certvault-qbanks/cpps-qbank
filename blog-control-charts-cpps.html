Run Charts, Control Charts, and Measures: CPPS Domain 4 Made Simple

Measure types, variation, and chart rules: the Domain 4 essentials, explained without the statistics headache.

6 min read • September 2026

In this article

  2. Two kinds of variation
  3. Reading a run chart
  4. Reading a control chart

  6. Practice questions

Performance Measurement, Analysis, Improvement and Monitoring is 25% of the scored CPPS exam, and it's where many candidates without a quality background lose points. You don't need to be a statistician. You need to recognize a few patterns quickly, often without scratch paper if you test remotely.

The four measure types

TypeWhat it tells youFalls prevention example
StructureWhether the resources and setup existNumber of low beds available per unit
ProcessWhether the right steps are happening% of patients with a fall risk assessment within 24 hours of admission
OutcomeWhether patients are better offFalls with injury per 1,000 patient days
BalancingWhether the change caused a problem somewhere elseRestraint use, or loss of mobility in patients kept in bed

Balancing measures are the ones candidates forget. When a question asks how to confirm that an improvement didn't create new harm, that's the answer.

Two kinds of variation

Every process varies. The key skill is telling which variation matters.

  • Common cause variation is the normal, built-in noise of a stable process. To reduce it, you have to change the process itself.
  • Special cause variation comes from something outside the usual system, like a new drug shortage or a staffing change. It's a signal worth investigating.

Don't tamper

Reacting to every up-and-down point as if it meant something is called tampering. It usually makes performance worse. If a chart shows only common cause variation, the right move is to redesign the process, not to chase individual data points.

Reading a run chart

A run chart plots data over time with a median line. Four rules, commonly taught by IHI, identify non-random signals:

  1. Shift: six or more consecutive points all above or all below the median. Points exactly on the median don't count toward or break the run.
  2. Trend: five or more consecutive points all going up or all going down.
  3. Too few or too many runs: a run is a series of points on one side of the median. A reference table gives the expected range for your number of points.
  4. Astronomical data point: a value obviously different from the rest.

Run charts are simple and need fewer data points, which makes them ideal early in an improvement project.

Reading a control chart

A control chart (also called a Shewhart chart) plots data around a center line, usually the mean, with upper and lower control limits typically set about three standard deviations away. Control limits come from the data. They aren't targets or specification limits.

Common rules for special cause include:

  • A single point outside a control limit
  • Eight or more consecutive points on one side of the center line
  • Six consecutive points steadily increasing or decreasing
  • Two out of three consecutive points near a control limit (in the outer third)
  • Fifteen consecutive points hugging the center line (in the inner third)

Rule sets vary slightly by source, so focus on the logic: a pattern too unlikely to be random noise is a signal.

Practice questions

Practice question 1

A unit launches a hand-hygiene program. The run chart shows seven consecutive weeks above the baseline median after launch. What does this indicate?

  1. Random variation; keep monitoring
  2. A shift, suggesting a non-random improvement
  3. A trend, suggesting the process is unstable
  4. An astronomical data point
Show answer

Answer: B. Six or more consecutive points on one side of the median is a shift, a signal that something changed.

Practice question 2

A team reducing catheter use wants to make sure the change isn't causing harm elsewhere. Which is the best balancing measure?

  1. Catheter days per 1,000 patient days
  2. CAUTI rate
  3. Rate of urinary retention requiring reinsertion
  4. % of catheters with a documented indication
Show answer

Answer: C. Removing catheters earlier could cause retention and reinsertion. That's the unintended consequence to watch. The others are outcome or process measures for the project itself.

Practice question 3

A control chart of monthly falls per 1,000 patient days shows all points within the control limits and no special cause patterns, but the rate is higher than leadership wants. What is the best next step?

  1. Investigate the highest month to find its cause
  2. Redesign the fall prevention process
  3. Change the control limits to match the target
  4. Wait for a point outside the limits before acting
Show answer

Answer: B. The process is stable but performing poorly. That's common cause variation, so improvement requires changing the system. Investigating one month would be tampering.
